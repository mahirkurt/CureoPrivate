## Luxury naming territory constraints

Luxury brands operate within tighter naming constraints than mass-market brands. Customer expectations shaped by 200+ years of luxury naming conventions are real and consequential.

### Convention 1: Founder name precedent

Many heritage luxury brands carry founder names (Hermès, Cartier, Patek Philippe, Loro Piana, Bottega Veneta — the founder reference of "Veneta" being geographic, but the family name pattern is the bedrock).

For new luxury brands:
- **Founder name acceptable**: gives heritage feel, anchors authenticity
- **Founder + place** acceptable: emphasizes provenance
- **Coined names**: acceptable only when positioning is "avant-garde luxury" or "modern luxury"
- **Generic descriptors**: not acceptable

### Convention 2: Heritage signaling

Luxury names often signal heritage even when newly founded:
- Latin/French/Italian linguistic roots
- Names that "could have been" old (timeless, not era-marked)
- Avoidance of tech-startup naming patterns
- Avoidance of casual/playful registers

### Convention 3: Restraint

Luxury names typically:
- Avoid hyperbole
- Avoid superlative claims in the name
- Sustain dignity at every pronunciation
- Read well across formal contexts

A luxury name that reads as marketing slang has failed.

### Convention 4: Classical forms

Even contemporary luxury names lean classical in form:
- Proper nouns (founder, place)
- Latinate or Romance-language phonetics
- Restraint in syllable count (typically 2-4 syllables)
- Avoidance of made-up letter combinations that signal "tech startup"

### Convention 5: Localization care

Luxury brands operate globally. Names must:
- Pronounce reasonably across major markets (EN, FR, IT, DE, CN, JP, AR)
- Carry consistent connotation across major markets
- Not collide with existing meanings in major markets

### Convention 6: Trademark defensibility

Luxury counterfeit market is enormous. Trademark protection is especially important. Names must:
- Be distinctive enough to defend strongly
- Cover Nice classes 14 (jewelry), 18 (leather goods), 25 (clothing), 35 (retail), 43 (hospitality) — depending on offering
- Be filed in jurisdictions where luxury counterfeits commonly originate

## Naming pattern recommendations for luxury

### Recommended patterns

1. **Founder surname**: "Hermès" pattern. Maximum heritage feel.
2. **Founder + descriptor**: "Patek Philippe" pattern. Two surnames or surname + descriptor.
3. **Place + craft**: "Bottega Veneta" pattern. Workshop + region.
4. **Classical noun**: "Loro Piana" — surnames that sound like classical references.
5. **Latinate coinage**: rare but defensible if executed with restraint.

### Disrecommended patterns

1. **Verb-derived names**: "Click", "Tap", "Buy" — energetic but anti-luxury
2. **Tech-startup phonetics**: "ify", "ly", "able" suffixes
3. **Acronyms**: unless founder initials with heritage (like BMW, where it works because of decades of equity)
4. **Generic descriptors**: "Premium", "Elite", "Exclusive" — claiming luxury rather than being it
5. **Playful registers**: jester-archetype names rarely sustain luxury positioning
6. **Hyperbolic claims**: "Best", "Finest" in the name itself

## Luxury naming sub-types

### Super-luxury
- Heritage founder names dominate
- New super-luxury brands often invent founder-style names
- Pronunciation effort acceptable (customers learn pronunciation; learning becomes part of belonging)

### Luxury (mid-tier luxury)
- Founder names common
- Coined names acceptable if classical-feeling
- Easier pronunciation more common

### Affordable luxury
- More naming flexibility
- Modern coined names acceptable
- Some tech-startup naming patterns work here (Glossier, Allbirds — affordable luxury that successfully uses contemporary naming)

### Avant-garde luxury (Off-White, Vetements, Ambush)
- Naming conventions deliberately inverted
- Oppositional, declarative, sometimes provocative
- Permission for unconventional forms

## How brand-maker applies these constraints

When luxury opt-in is active:

1. **Step 1: Strategic Decoding** — confirm luxury tier (super / luxury / affordable / avant-garde); adapts the constraint stringency
2. **Step 2: Five-Category Brainstorm** — biases:
   - Category A (Descriptive): largely disabled (luxury rarely descriptive)
   - Category B (Associative): heritage-referential, place-referential
   - Category C (Coined): only if classical-feeling
   - Category D (Founder): heavily emphasized
   - Category E (Abstract): only if heritage-feeling
3. **Step 3: SMILE+M Lab** — adjusted weights (heritage signaling matters more than sonic playfulness)
4. **Step 4: Live Domain Check** — luxury domain availability often tighter; check .com + country-code TLDs aggressively
5. **Step 5: Post-Digital Validation** — additional luxury-specific checks:
   - Pronunciation across luxury markets (EN, FR, IT, CN, AR)
   - Counterfeit-defensibility (distinctiveness threshold)
   - Cultural-meaning check (does the name signal something unintended in major luxury markets?)
   - Tier-fit check (does the name feel super-luxury vs affordable luxury appropriately?)

## Luxury bilingual considerations

Many luxury brands operate in French/Italian/EN simultaneously. Naming should:

- Work across the brand's primary languages
- Not require translation (luxury brands rarely localize names)
- Maintain consistent connotation across pronunciation in each language
- Survive the AR / CN / RU pronunciation environments where luxury demand is high

## Final naming finalists for luxury

Per brand-maker's standard 3-5 finalist output, luxury finalists also include:

- Tier-fit assessment per candidate (super-luxury appropriate, luxury appropriate, affordable luxury appropriate)
- Multi-market pronunciation notes (EN, FR, IT, CN, AR at minimum)
- Counterfeit-defensibility assessment
- Founder-style vs coined balance (most luxury portfolios benefit from at least 2 founder-style candidates in the finalists set)

## Anti-patterns specific to luxury naming

| Anti-pattern | Symptom |
|---|---|
| Tech-startup naming for luxury | Brand names "Luxify"; positioning fails immediately |
| Hyperbolic in-name claims | "Premium Excellence Co" — claims luxury rather than being it |
| Tier mismatch | Super-luxury positioning, affordable-luxury naming register |
| Counterfeit-vulnerable name | Generic name impossible to defend against luxury counterfeit |
| Translation needed | Name requires translation in major luxury markets, signals non-heritage |
| Casual register | Playful name in super-luxury context; positioning fragility |
