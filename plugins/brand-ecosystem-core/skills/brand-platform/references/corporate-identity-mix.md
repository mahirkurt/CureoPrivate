# Corporate Identity Mix — B2B/Enterprise Opt-In Module (Foroudi/Olins)

**Activation triggers**: "B2B", "enterprise", "professional services", "consultancy", "law firm", "accounting", "management consulting", "industrial", "infrastructure", "logistics", "manufacturing", "wholesale".

When triggered, this module overlays the academic corporate identity framework on the standard platform. Without trigger, this module is silent.

## Origin

Foroudi, Mahdavi & Foroudi's *Corporate Brand Design: Developing and Managing Brand Identity* synthesizes 40+ years of corporate identity research from Wally Olins' foundational work (*The Corporate Personality*, 1978) through contemporary multi-disciplinary frameworks. The module operationalizes:

- The corporate identity mix (Mind/Soul/Voice × Behaviour/Communication/Symbolism)
- Social identity theory applied to corporate context
- Stakeholder map beyond customers
- Identity vs Image vs Reputation distinction

## Why corporate B2B brands need a different overlay

Consumer brand frameworks center the customer-brand dyad. Corporate brands operate in a multi-stakeholder field — customers are one constituency among many. A B2B technology brand serves:
- End-users (who experience the product)
- Buyers (who write the cheque)
- Influencers (analysts, journalists, advisors)
- Employees (whose identification with the brand affects retention)
- Investors (who fund the operations)
- Partners (whose endorsement enables distribution)
- Regulators (whose constraints shape what's possible)
- Community (whose tolerance enables the social license to operate)

Each constituency reads the brand differently. The corporate identity mix provides a framework for ensuring coherent presentation across all of them.

## The Corporate Identity Mix

### Three identity axes

Following Balmer's framework (cited in Foroudi):

- **Mind** — what the corporation consciously decides: managerial vision, strategy, product performance, corporate philosophy, corporate history
- **Soul** — what the corporation subjectively is: beliefs shared by employees, sub-cultural mix, organizational mood
- **Voice** — how the corporation communicates: total corporate communication across all channels and stakeholders

### Three expression modes

Following He & Balmer's framework:

- **Behaviour** — what the corporation does (decisions, actions, conduct)
- **Communication** — what the corporation says (formal and informal communication outputs)
- **Symbolism** — what the corporation symbolizes (visual identity, language, ritual)

### The 3×3 matrix

The corporate identity mix is the intersection of these axes:

|  | Behaviour | Communication | Symbolism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mind** | Strategic actions | Strategic announcements | Strategic visual cues |
| **Soul** | Cultural behaviors | Internal narratives | Cultural symbols |
| **Voice** | Service delivery | Marketing communications | Brand identity system |

For a corporate B2B brand to present coherently, all nine cells must align.

## Module Additions to Platform Document

When activated, the corporate B2B module adds:

### Addition 1 — Stakeholder Map (replaces simple persona)

The single-persona block of Element 5 is extended to a stakeholder map. Standard B2B stakeholder list:

| Stakeholder | Primary need | What they read in the brand |
|---|---|---|
| End-user | Job to be done | Usability, reliability |
| Buyer / Procurement | Risk + ROI | Credibility, fiscal responsibility |
| Influencer (analyst, advisor, journalist) | Independent assessment | Vision + execution credibility |
| Employee | Identification | Culture + meaning |
| Investor | Return | Strategy + execution + market position |
| Partner | Channel viability | Stability + co-marketing capacity |
| Regulator | Compliance | Compliance posture, transparency |
| Community | Social license | Ethics, contribution |

The corporate B2B platform must produce a sub-persona for each Primary stakeholder. Secondary stakeholders receive shorter treatments.

### Addition 2 — Identity vs Image vs Reputation Distinction

A consumer brand often conflates these. Corporate brands cannot:

- **Identity** — what the corporation actually is (Soul + Mind, internally)
- **Image** — how the corporation is perceived at a moment in time (the result of communication × audience interpretation)
- **Reputation** — what the corporation is known for over time (the accumulated impression)

The platform document distinguishes:
- **Identity statement** — internal truth (this is who we are)
- **Image objectives** — desired perception (this is what we want them to see at the moment of decision)
- **Reputation aspirations** — long-term what-we-want-to-be-known-for

The three should align but are not identical.

### Addition 3 — Corporate Brand Signature

For corporate brands, the brand signature is a specific structured relationship between:
- Corporate name (the company)
- Brandmark (the visual identifier)
- Descriptor (what the company does — sometimes required by regulators)
- Tagline (optional)

The corporate brand signature is a more formal construct than a consumer brand signature. The platform module produces signature specifications including:

- Long form (full name + descriptor + brandmark)
- Short form (name + brandmark)
- Brand-only form (brandmark alone)
- Locked relationships between elements
- Hierarchy when multiple corporate entities co-exist

### Addition 4 — Brand Architecture Decision

Corporate brands face a brand architecture question more acutely than consumer brands:

- **Monolithic** — one master brand, sub-products endorsed (FedEx, IBM, Virgin)
- **Endorsed** — sub-brands with parent endorsement (Nestlé KitKat, Microsoft Xbox)
- **Pluralistic / House of Brands** — independent sub-brands, parent invisible (Procter & Gamble, Unilever)

The platform module produces an explicit brand architecture declaration with rationale.

### Addition 5 — Corporate Voice vs Customer-Facing Voice

A corporate B2B brand often runs two voices in parallel:
- **Corporate voice** — to investors, regulators, partners
- **Customer-facing voice** — to buyers and users

These must be related (sharing core values) but tonally distinct. The platform module produces specifications for both voices, with rules for where each applies.

## Stakeholder Persona Construction

For each Primary stakeholder, construct a sub-persona with:

1. **Role** — what they do in their organization
2. **Decision criteria** — what they look for when evaluating the brand
3. **Risk profile** — what they fear
4. **Information sources** — where they form opinions
5. **Brand expectations** — what they expect the brand to be

Example for "Buyer / Procurement" in a B2B SaaS context:

> **Role**: VP Procurement or category-specialist purchasing manager at a mid-to-large enterprise
> **Decision criteria**: Vendor financial stability, contractual terms, security and compliance posture, references from comparable customers, total cost of ownership across 3-5 year horizon
> **Risk profile**: Choosing a vendor that fails, that gets acquired by a competitor, that increases prices punitively at renewal, that suffers a security breach
> **Information sources**: Analyst reports (Gartner, Forrester), peer references via private networks, vendor security questionnaires, RFP responses
> **Brand expectations**: Professional, credible, stable, transparent about limitations, responsive to procurement-specific questions

## Reputation Aspiration Discipline

The corporate B2B platform declares 3 reputation pillars — what the brand wants to be known for over 5-10 years. Each pillar:

- Must be operationally true today (or credibly buildable)
- Must be defensible (the brand must be able to compete on this pillar)
- Must matter to Primary stakeholders

Common reputation pillars by sector:
- Technology B2B: technical excellence, customer success, security posture
- Professional services: depth of expertise, trusted advisor relationship, results delivery
- Industrial: reliability, safety, long-term partnership
- Financial services: stability, transparency, performance

## How the Corporate B2B Module Affects Downstream

- **brand-story**: Multi-stakeholder narrative — same brand promise delivered with different framing to different stakeholders
- **brand-maker**: Corporate names tend toward authority, restraint, founder-name precedent; avoid playful coinages
- **brand-visual**: Corporate identity systems demand precision and longevity; design language tilts toward Logo Modernism / Swiss tradition; favicons and presentation systems prioritized
- **brand-touchpoint**: Stationery and proposal documents become hero touchpoints; events and conferences elevated; uniforms or dress-code specifications for client-facing teams
- **brand-launch**: Internal-first launch becomes near-mandatory; external launch is analyst-relations-driven; PR + thought leadership before advertising
