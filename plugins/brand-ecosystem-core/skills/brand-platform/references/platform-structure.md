# Platform Structure — The Eight Elements

This reference is **mandatory** (Step 0). It defines the structural anatomy of the Brand Platform document.

## Origin of the eight-element structure

The structure synthesizes:
- Geyrhalter's Brand Platform doctrine (vision, values, positioning, brand personality, target audience)
- Johnson's "Investigate" phase outputs (audit findings, audience definition, positioning territory)
- Wheeler's Phase 1+2 outputs (brand brief, audience, competitive context, brand essence)
- Foroudi corporate identity mix overlay (mind/soul/voice for corporate B2B brands)

The eight elements are non-negotiable. Skipping any element produces a platform that cannot fully serve downstream skills.

## The Eight Elements

### 1. Vision

**Definition**: The aspirational endpoint — what the world looks like when this brand has succeeded at its mission.

**Discipline**:
- Single sentence, ≤25 words
- Future-state, present-tense ("we envision a world where…", not "we want to…")
- Outlives any individual product or strategy pivot
- Not a marketing slogan

**Quality test**: If you replaced every product the brand currently sells with a different product line, would the vision still hold? If yes, it's a vision. If no, it's a roadmap.

**Examples (synthesized, not literal)**:
- Patagonia (paraphrased): *A world where every economic decision considers the planet first.*
- Tesla (paraphrased): *A world running on sustainable energy.*
- IKEA (paraphrased): *A better everyday life for the many people.*

### 2. Mission

**Definition**: Why the brand exists today and what it does for customers, operationally.

**Discipline**:
- Single sentence, ≤30 words
- Present-tense, verb-led
- Names what the brand does + for whom + by what mechanism
- Specific enough to falsify

**Quality test**: Does the mission read as `<verb> + <beneficiary> + <mechanism>`? If those three components aren't clearly identifiable, rewrite.

### 3. Core Values (3–5)

**Definition**: Behavioral principles the brand and its people live by, internally and externally.

**Discipline**:
- 3 minimum, 5 maximum
- Single word or short phrase per value, not a sentence
- Pass the **opposite test**: the opposite must be a position some other brand could plausibly hold
- Each value translates to specific operational behaviors (defined in Step 8)

**The opposite test in practice**:
- "Integrity" — opposite is "dishonesty". No brand holds "dishonesty" as a value. → Empty filler, drop it.
- "Speed" — opposite is "deliberateness". A craft luxury brand might hold "deliberateness" as a value. → Real, keep it.
- "Quality" — opposite is "good enough". A mass-market brand might hold "good enough at lower cost" as a value. → Real, keep it.
- "Innovation" — opposite is "stability". A 200-year-old private bank might hold "stability" as a value. → Real, keep it.

### 4. Brand Personality (three-layer composite)

**Definition**: Who the brand is as a person, expressed in three coordinated layers.

**Layer A — Jung archetype**:
One primary archetype, optionally a secondary archetype. The 12 archetypes per Pearson & Mark:

| Archetype | Driver | Brand voice tendency |
|---|---|---|
| Sage | Truth | Knowledgeable, considered |
| Outlaw | Liberation | Defiant, raw |
| Creator | Self-expression | Artistic, ambitious |
| Hero | Mastery | Bold, brave |
| Caregiver | Service | Warm, compassionate |
| Magician | Power | Transformative, visionary |
| Everyman | Belonging | Approachable, fair |
| Lover | Intimacy | Sensual, devoted |
| Jester | Joy | Playful, irreverent |
| Ruler | Control | Authoritative, refined |
| Innocent | Safety | Optimistic, pure |
| Explorer | Freedom | Adventurous, restless |

A brand may have a secondary archetype that modulates the primary. *Innocent + Caregiver* reads very differently from *Innocent + Sage*.

**Layer B — Aaker 5-dimension polygon**:
Score the brand 0–10 on each:
- **Sincerity**: down-to-earth, honest, wholesome, cheerful
- **Excitement**: daring, spirited, imaginative, up-to-date
- **Competence**: reliable, intelligent, successful
- **Sophistication**: upper-class, charming
- **Ruggedness**: outdoorsy, tough

Two brands sharing the Magician archetype can differ sharply — one high on Sophistication and Competence (Apple), one high on Excitement and Ruggedness (Red Bull).

**Layer C — Voice traits**:
3–5 concrete adjectives describing the brand's verbal register. These are NOT synonyms; each adjective must add new dimension.

Example for a craft coffee brand:
- Curious (not "interesting")
- Plainspoken (not "simple")
- Generous (not "warm")

### 5. Target Persona

**Definition**: A composite ideal customer who the brand serves with full force.

**Demographic block** — answer each:
- Age range
- Gender (if salient)
- Income band
- Geography
- Education
- Occupation cluster
- Household composition (if salient)
- Major life stage (if salient)

**Psychographic block** — write a paragraph (150–250 words) describing:
- Values they hold
- Attitudes they carry
- A typical day in their life
- Media habits and content diet
- Brand affinities (in and outside the category)
- Unmet needs the category currently fails to address

**Segment placement** — pick one of Young & Rubicam's 4Cs:

| Segment | % of population | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Resigned | 10% | Older, brand-loyal, value-oriented, prefers familiar |
| Struggler | 8% | Younger, sensation-seeking, brand-loyal to escape-offering brands |
| Mainstreamer | 30% | Conventional, family-focused, risk-averse |
| Aspirer | 13% | Status-seeking, materialistic, image-driven |
| Succeeder | 16% | Confident, goal-oriented, premium-affinity |
| Explorer | 9% | Discovery-driven, early-adopter, novelty-seeking |
| Reformer | 14% | Intellectually-driven, socially-conscious, principles-led |

**Maslow layer placement**:
- Physiological needs (food, water, shelter, sleep)
- Safety (security, employment, resources)
- Love and belonging (friendship, family, intimacy)
- Esteem (respect, status, recognition, freedom)
- Self-actualization (creativity, purpose, growth)

The brand's primary Maslow level dictates the emotional register of all downstream communications. A brand operating at "self-actualization" speaks very differently from one at "safety".

### 6. Positioning Statement (six-clause Ries-Trout formula)

**The formula**:
```
For <target customer>,
who <unmet need or problem>,
<Brand> is the <category>
that <key benefit or point of difference>,
unlike <primary competitor>,
because <reason to believe>.
```

**Discipline per clause**:
- **For**: Be specific. "For people who want better coffee" fails. "For independent professionals who treat coffee as a daily ritual rather than a caffeine delivery system" passes.
- **Who**: State the unmet need from the customer's perspective, not the brand's vocabulary.
- **Is the**: Choose category carefully. The category you claim is the category you'll be compared against.
- **That**: One benefit, one point of difference. Multiple benefits = no benefit.
- **Unlike**: Name a specific primary competitor. "Unlike other coffee brands" fails. "Unlike Starbucks" passes.
- **Because**: Substantiate with evidence — heritage, technology, sourcing, methodology, founder credibility.

**Why this matters**: Every downstream skill reads this clause-by-clause. A vague positioning statement produces vague naming, vague visual identity, vague launch.

### 7. Brand Promise

**Definition**: What the customer can expect to experience every time they engage the brand.

**Format**: `When you choose <Brand>, you experience <transformation>.`

**Discipline**:
- ≤20 words
- Customer-pointed (not "we deliver…" but "you experience…")
- Operationally deliverable across every touchpoint
- Specific enough to falsify

**Quality test**: Can the brand fail to deliver this promise? If the promise is unfalsifiable ("you experience excellence"), it's marketing fluff. If it can be falsified ("you receive your order within 48 hours"), it's a real promise.

### 8. Brand Voice Principles

**Definition**: 3–5 principles governing how the brand speaks — in copy, in customer service, in keynote presentations, in social media replies.

**Structure of each principle**:
- Positive directive (what the voice does)
- Negative directive (what the voice never does)
- Specimen pair: "yes" example + "no" example

**Example**:
> **Principle**: Concrete over abstract.
> *Yes*: "Ships in 48 hours from our Brooklyn workshop."
> *No*: "Industry-leading delivery times."

The voice principles are the bridge between brand strategy and brand operations. They're the first thing a new copywriter or customer service agent needs to internalize.

## Element Sequencing — Why this order matters

Vision → Mission → Values → Personality → Persona → Positioning → Promise → Voice

This order is causal:
- Vision provides direction → Mission provides current operation → Values provide behavioral guardrails
- Personality + Persona together establish the dyadic relationship
- Positioning emerges from the intersection of Personality, Persona, and Competition
- Promise is the customer-pointed face of Positioning
- Voice is the operational tool that delivers Promise consistently

Producing these out of order risks circular reasoning: defining voice before personality, defining positioning before audience, defining values before vision.

## The Geyrhalter SAS test (final gate)

Before finalizing the platform, validate against Geyrhalter's three-part test:

- **Soul**: Does the brand have a reason for being beyond profit? Is there something a customer can connect to emotionally? If not, the brand has no soul — it's a product line.
- **Attractive**: Has the brand been designed with intention — values curated, voice carefully selected, identity considered? If not, the brand is generic.
- **Smart**: Is the brand operationally usable? Can the team execute it? Can customers engage with it easily? If not, the brand is a fantasy document.

A brand failing SAS is not yet ready for downstream skills. Return to the failing element and remediate.
