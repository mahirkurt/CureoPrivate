# Positioning Doctrine — Ries-Trout Operationalized

This reference is **mandatory** (Step 0). It governs Element 6 (Positioning Statement) of the platform document.

## Origin

Al Ries and Jack Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981) established the canonical doctrine: positioning is not what you do to the product; positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect. Forty-five years later this remains the operative principle. Modern additions (Aaker's positioning grids, Sharp's "mental availability", Ehrenberg-Bass Institute research) refine but do not replace it.

## The Six-Clause Formula

```
For <target customer>,
who <unmet need or problem>,
<Brand> is the <category>
that <key benefit or point of difference>,
unlike <primary competitor>,
because <reason to believe>.
```

This is the deliverable. Every clause is mandatory. The formula appears mechanical but each clause is a strategic choice with consequences.

## Clause-by-Clause Doctrine

### Clause 1: "For <target customer>"

**The error to avoid**: Generic targeting ("for everyone", "for consumers who care about quality").

**The discipline**: Target customer must be specific enough that the audience exclusion is obvious. If you can't name three customer segments that the brand is *not* for, the targeting is too broad.

**Tests**:
- Can a brand strategist sketch this customer in three sentences?
- Would two different writers, given the positioning statement, draw the same customer?
- Is the customer described by behavior + context, not just demographics?

**Examples of specificity**:
- Weak: "For young professionals"
- Stronger: "For independent professionals who treat coffee as a daily ritual"
- Strongest: "For independent professionals working from home, age 28–42, urban, who treat coffee as a daily ritual rather than a caffeine delivery system"

### Clause 2: "Who <unmet need or problem>"

**The error to avoid**: Stating the brand's solution capability as if it were the customer's need.

**The discipline**: State the need from the customer's vocabulary. The customer does not think in terms of the brand's product categories. They think in terms of problems they are trying to solve and feelings they are trying to escape or attain.

**Use Miller's three-layer problem analysis here** (Step 1 in `brand-story` will go deeper):
- External problem: the observable thing in the world
- Internal problem: the emotional frustration
- Philosophical problem: the "this is wrong in the world" stance

For the positioning statement, capture primarily the external + internal. The philosophical layer enters elsewhere.

### Clause 3: "<Brand> is the <category>"

**The error to avoid**: Choosing the obvious category, which is also the saturated category.

**The discipline**: Category claim is a strategic decision, not a description. Three options:

1. **Established category, defensible position**: Operate in a known category but claim a defensible position within it ("the considered alternative to mass coffee").
2. **Category subdivision**: Define a sub-category the brand owns ("specialty single-origin coffee, brewed to roaster spec").
3. **Category creation**: Define a new category entirely ("ritual coffee" — a category that previously did not exist).

Each path has different downstream consequences. Established category accepts existing competitive frame. Subdivision asks the customer to learn a sub-distinction. Creation demands market education investment.

**Test**: Search for the chosen category online. If thousands of results saturate the term, it's an established category. If a handful of results appear, it's a subdivision. If almost no results appear, it's category creation.

### Clause 4: "That <key benefit or point of difference>"

**The error to avoid**: Multiple benefits. "That offers superior quality, exceptional service, and great value" = no benefit.

**The discipline**: One benefit, one point of difference. The strongest positioning statements pick a single dimension and own it.

**Use Wheeler's brand essence distillation here**: if you cannot reduce the benefit to a single phrase, the strategy is not yet clear.

**Examples**:
- Weak: "That offers high-quality products and excellent service"
- Stronger: "That ships within 48 hours, every time"
- Stronger still: "That guarantees 48-hour delivery on every order"

### Clause 5: "Unlike <primary competitor>"

**The error to avoid**: "Unlike other brands in our category". This is positioning against air.

**The discipline**: Name a specific primary competitor. Naming a competitor is uncomfortable; it forces the brand to take a position. That discomfort is the point.

**Selection criteria for the named competitor**:
- The competitor the target customer most often chooses instead
- A competitor large enough that the comparison signals ambition
- A competitor whose position the brand can credibly distinguish itself from

**Direct mention rule**: The competitor named in this clause is the competitor the brand will be compared against forever. Choose wisely.

### Clause 6: "Because <reason to believe>"

**The error to avoid**: Aspirational substantiation that the brand has not yet earned.

**The discipline**: Reason to believe must be operationally true today, not a promise of future capability. The customer will encounter this evidence; if it doesn't exist, the positioning collapses.

**Sources of legitimate reason to believe**:
- **Heritage**: founding date, methodology lineage, association with credentialed individuals
- **Technology**: proprietary process, patented mechanism, measurable performance
- **Sourcing**: origin specificity, partnership with named producers, certifications
- **Methodology**: documented approach, repeatable framework, named system
- **Founder credibility**: relevant expertise, public record, recognized authority
- **Operational evidence**: production capacity, distribution footprint, service infrastructure
- **Social proof**: customer base scale, named customers, third-party validation

## The Category Ladder

Ries-Trout's "category ladder" doctrine: in any category, the customer's mind holds a ranked ladder of brands. The first brand becomes the category-defining anchor; subsequent brands compete for positions 2, 3, 4 with diminishing returns.

**Strategic implication**: Three positioning options for a new brand:
1. **Category leader** (existing or claimed): Position the brand as #1 in an existing category (risky if category leader exists with substantial mind share).
2. **Against the leader**: Define identity in opposition to the established leader. "We're the X for people who don't want Y." Avis vs Hertz. Apple vs IBM.
3. **New category**: Create the category. First brand in a new category is automatically #1.

The choice influences every clause of the positioning statement.

## Modern Refinements (post-2010)

### Byron Sharp's "mental availability"

Ehrenberg-Bass Institute research argues brands grow primarily through reach and mental availability, not differentiation. This does not refute positioning — it qualifies it. A positioning statement gives the brand a *coherent identity* to make mentally available. Without positioning, you cannot consistently build mental availability.

### Sharp's "category entry points" (CEPs)

A modern positioning statement should be aware of the CEPs — the cognitive triggers that bring the category to mind. A brand whose positioning matches a high-frequency CEP gets recalled more often. This influences Clause 2 (unmet need): identify which CEP the brand activates.

### Wolff Olins "fluid identity" era

In an adaptive identity era (also relevant to brand-visual Route 4), positioning can be modular: a core position + contextual variants. This does not weaken Ries-Trout; it acknowledges that the *expression* of positioning may vary by context while the *core* remains stable.

## Bilingual Discipline

The positioning statement is one of the few platform elements that benefits from explicit bilingual treatment.

**Why**: Because positioning statements are read by leadership, by agency partners, by employees, by translators of customer-facing materials. Drift between EN and TR versions causes downstream message inconsistency.

**Format**:

```
EN:
For <target>,
who <need>,
<Brand> is the <category>
that <benefit>,
unlike <competitor>,
because <reason>.

TR:
<Hedef> için,
ki <ihtiyaç>,
<Marka>, <kategori> olarak konumlanır,
çünkü <fayda>,
<Rakip> ile karşılaştırıldığında,
zira <neden>.
```

Both versions must be reviewed for strategic equivalence, not literal translation. A word-for-word translation often loses the strategic point.

## Quality Gates

Before passing the positioning statement downstream, validate:

1. **Specificity check**: Can a strategist sketch the customer from Clause 1 alone? If not, Clause 1 fails.
2. **Customer-vocabulary check**: Does Clause 2 use language the customer would use, or language the brand uses? If the latter, rewrite.
3. **Single-benefit check**: Does Clause 4 contain one or multiple benefits? If multiple, choose one.
4. **Named-competitor check**: Does Clause 5 name a specific competitor? If "other brands" or unnamed, rewrite.
5. **Falsifiability check**: Could the reason in Clause 6 be objectively disproven? If unfalsifiable, replace with something operationally true.
6. **Robin-to-Batman check**: Does the positioning statement center the customer or the brand? If the brand appears as hero, rewrite.

## Common Failure Modes

| Failure | Symptom | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Generic targeting | Clause 1 could fit any brand | Add behavioral + contextual specificity |
| Brand-vocabulary problem | Clause 2 uses category jargon | Rewrite in customer language |
| Saturated category claim | Clause 3 has thousands of competitors | Subdivide or create category |
| Multi-benefit overflow | Clause 4 has commas connecting benefits | Pick one |
| Phantom competitor | Clause 5 names "the market" | Name a real company |
| Aspirational substantiation | Clause 6 promises future capability | Replace with current truth |
| Brand-as-hero | Whole statement reads as brand bragging | Rewrite from customer perspective |
