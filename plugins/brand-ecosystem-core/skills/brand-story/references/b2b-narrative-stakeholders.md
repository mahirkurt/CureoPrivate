## 2. The Multi-Stakeholder Problem

In consumer narrative, "the customer" is a relatively unified figure — even if research reveals psychographic complexity, the buying decision sits with one person or household unit. In B2B, this collapses immediately upon contact with reality.

A typical enterprise software purchase involves:
- **Economic Buyer**: signs the contract, owns the budget (CFO, VP Procurement)
- **Technical Buyer**: validates fit, often issues gating veto (CTO, IT Director)
- **User Buyer**: will live with the product (line manager, individual contributor)
- **Champion**: advocates internally (mid-level sponsor)
- **Influencer**: shapes perception (analyst, advisor, consultant)
- **Gatekeeper**: controls access to others (executive assistant, procurement officer)

Each of these stakeholders has **different problems, different success criteria, different anxieties**. A monolithic BrandScript cannot serve them all. B2B narrative requires a **stakeholder map** with role-specific BrandScript variants linked through a shared philosophical layer.

---

## 3. Stakeholder Mapping Method

For each B2B narrative engagement, produce a stakeholder map with at minimum:

| Role | External Problem | Internal Problem | Philosophical Problem | Success Definition | Failure Definition |
|------|-----------------|------------------|----------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Economic Buyer | Cost overrun, ROI shortfall | Career risk on budget commitment | Resource stewardship | Project comes in under budget with documented ROI | Forced to defend a failed investment to board |
| Technical Buyer | Integration failure, security gap | Technical reputation at risk | Engineering excellence | Solution integrates cleanly, scales | Production incident traced to procurement decision |
| User Buyer | Daily workflow friction | Sense of being unheard in tool selection | Dignity of craft | Daily work becomes easier | Tool imposed from above that worsens daily work |
| Champion | Lack of leverage with peers | Reputation as the one who recommended | Organizational competence | Endorsement is vindicated | Public backing of a failed project |
| Influencer | Recommending the wrong vendor | Authority undermined | Industry stewardship | Advisory thesis vindicated by outcome | Recommendation backfires publicly |
| Gatekeeper | Wasted exec time | Trust with principal | Effective filtering | Principal's time well-protected | Principal complains about wasted hour |

Each row defines a stakeholder-specific BrandScript fragment. The fragments **must align** on the philosophical layer (shared organizational worldview the brand promises) but may differ at external, internal, plan, and CTA layers.

---

## 4. Philosophical Layer Alignment

The philosophical layer is the **convergence point**. Where consumer narrative's philosophical layer is often a personal worldview ("life should reward attention"), B2B's philosophical layer is an **organizational worldview** ("organizations should compound their advantages, not their entropy").

Examples of B2B philosophical layers (each canonically grounded):

- "Decisions should compound, not regret." (advisory services)
- "Infrastructure should disappear when it works." (enterprise platform)
- "Expertise should be distributed, not bottlenecked." (knowledge platform)
- "Capital should know what it owns." (financial software)
- "Process should serve outcome, not perform itself." (operations consulting)

This layer is the **only** layer that appears identically across all stakeholder fragments. It is the brand's organizational thesis.

## 5. SB7 Element Modifications by Stakeholder

### 5.1 Character (Hero)

Each stakeholder is a hero in their own fragment. The narrative does not pick favorites; it builds parallel BrandScripts that all converge at the philosophical layer.

Anti-pattern: writing a BrandScript that only addresses the Economic Buyer (cost/ROI/value) while ignoring the User Buyer (daily workflow). This produces narratives that win RFPs but generate user resentment post-implementation, eroding renewal economics.

### 5.2 Problem

External and internal problems differ wildly by role. The Economic Buyer's external problem (budget defensibility) is invisible to the User Buyer (workflow friction).

The stakeholder map must enumerate role-specific external and internal problems, then identify the **philosophical convergence**.

### 5.3 Guide

Authority signals also differ by stakeholder:
- Economic Buyer: financial proof points, customer lifetime value math, board-credible analyst recognition
- Technical Buyer: technical certifications, architecture diagrams, references from technical peers
- User Buyer: user testimonials, usability research, peer-org case studies
- Champion: internal-facing tools, leverage artifacts ("show your colleagues this")
- Influencer: analyst-grade depth, thought-leadership artifacts
- Gatekeeper: efficient access (clear value prop in 30 seconds)

The Guide's authority is **modular** — different proof artifacts for different stakeholders, all rooted in the same organizational competence.

### 5.4 Plan

Procurement processes are themselves multi-step plans. B2B narrative aligns with the customer's actual procurement plan:
- Stage 1: Awareness → Plan element: "Recognize the category"
- Stage 2: Consideration → Plan element: "Evaluate against criteria"
- Stage 3: Validation → Plan element: "Confirm fit through pilot or proof"
- Stage 4: Commitment → Plan element: "Decide and contract"
- Stage 5: Implementation → Plan element: "Activate and onboard"
- Stage 6: Renewal → Plan element: "Compound and expand"

Each stage has stakeholder-specific narrative beats.

### 5.5 Call to Action

B2B CTAs map to procurement stage, not just stakeholder:
- Awareness: download category-defining content
- Consideration: technical product tour
- Validation: pilot agreement, proof of concept
- Commitment: contract, MSA
- Implementation: onboarding workshop
- Renewal: account expansion conversation

### 5.6 Failure

Failure stakes are organizationally framed:
- Economic Buyer: opportunity cost, board exposure
- Technical Buyer: technical debt accumulation, incident exposure
- User Buyer: workflow degradation, turnover risk
- Champion: internal credibility loss
- Influencer: published-thesis erosion

### 5.7 Success

Success states are equally role-specific. The unified narrative gathers them under the shared philosophical layer's promised future.

## 6. Voice Calibration for B2B

| Register | Consumer Default | B2B Overlay |
|----------|------------------|-------------|
| Specificity | Aspirational language fine | Specific outcomes required ("reduce cycle time by N days," not "make work easier") |
| Proof | Testimonial-driven | Quantified case study-driven |
| Tense | Present + future | Past tense for proof + present for thesis + future for outcome |
| Confidence | Bold claims acceptable | Measured claims with attribution required (analyst quote, customer name) |
| Emotional register | Warm | Restrained, professional, measured optimism |
| First person | "We" common | "We" + house-name ("Acme") + "our customers" all in rotation |
| Industry jargon | Avoid | Domain fluency expected at appropriate level (CFO content uses CFO language) |

## 7. IMC Plan Modifications for B2B

**Standard IMC Reimagined:** Internal storyteller program + external multi-channel diffusion.
**B2B overlay:**

| IMC Element | Consumer Standard | B2B Overlay |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Internal storyteller program | All employees as ambassadors | Sales + customer success + product marketing as primary storytellers; org-wide BrandScript training |
| Owned channels | Website, blog, social | Website (deep) + blog (thought leadership) + LinkedIn (executive presence) + email nurture sequences |
| Earned channels | PR, influencer | Analyst relations (Gartner, Forrester, IDC) + industry press + customer reference program |
| Paid channels | Performance marketing | Account-based marketing, LinkedIn Sponsored, industry trade media, conference sponsorships |
| Events | Activations, popups | Field events, hosted summits, partner events, customer advisory boards |
| Sales enablement | Light | Heavy — pitch decks, battle cards, ROI calculators, demo scripts all derive from BrandScript |
| Customer marketing | Optional | Mandatory — case study production pipeline, customer advocacy program, NPS-to-advocacy conversion |

## 8. Robin-to-Batman Test (B2B Modifier)

The standard Robin-to-Batman test asks: is the customer the hero (Batman) and the brand the Guide (Alfred)?

**B2B modifier:** Multiple Batmen exist (each stakeholder), each needing their own Alfred-orientation. The brand-as-Guide stance must be consistent across stakeholder fragments — never positioning the brand as hero in any fragment.

When auditing a B2B BrandScript, run the Robin-to-Batman test **per stakeholder fragment**. The brand must remain Guide in all of them. The most common failure: positioning the brand as hero to the Champion (helping the Champion win internal battles) — this subtly inverts the relationship.

---

## 9. Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Monolithic BrandScript** addressing only the Economic Buyer (cost-led) or only the User Buyer (UX-led)
- **Hero brand language**: "We revolutionize procurement." (B2B audiences are skeptical of self-aggrandizement.)
- **Vague philosophical layer**: "We help businesses grow." (Too generic to differentiate.)
- **Mismatched proof to stakeholder**: technical white paper sent to CFO; financial ROI sent to engineer
- **Missing renewal narrative**: B2B narratives that end at contract signature, not at value compounding through renewal
- **Champion as hero**: positioning the brand as helping the Champion win internal battles — inverts Guide relationship
