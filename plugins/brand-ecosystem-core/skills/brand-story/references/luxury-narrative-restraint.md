When activated, this module **overlays** the standard SB7 BrandScript work with luxury restraint discipline. The standard skill body still produces the seven-element BrandScript, but each element's tone, register, and visibility are recalibrated according to the rules below.

## 2. The Restraint Principle (Foundational Doctrine)

Luxury narrative does not "tell" — it implies. Where mass-market brands deploy overt customer-as-hero framing (Miller's SB7 in its assertive form), luxury narrative operates by **withholding**.

Derval's core insight (*Designing Luxury Brands*, Ch. 4): luxury customers are not won by being shown what they get; they are won by being shown what they are presumed to already know. The narrative therefore presupposes the customer's sophistication rather than performing it for them.

Practical consequences:
- Direct CTAs are softened to invitations
- Stakes are alluded to, not enumerated
- Failure scenarios are never depicted graphically
- The Guide does not introduce itself — its authority is taken as given

This is **not** narrative absence; it is narrative compression to its highest density.

## 3. SB7 Element Modifications (Element-by-Element Overlay)

### 3.1 Character (Hero)

**Standard SB7:** Customer named, problem articulated, desire stated.
**Luxury overlay:** Customer is referenced obliquely — by taste, lineage, milieu, sensibility — not by demographic or pain point. Hero is not "the busy executive" but "the connoisseur for whom time itself is currency."

Anti-pattern to avoid: customer aspiration framed as compensatory ("become the person you wish to be"). Luxury customers are presumed to already be that person.

### 3.2 Problem (Three Layers)

**Standard SB7:** External problem (tangible) + Internal problem (emotional) + Philosophical problem (worldview).
**Luxury overlay:** The external problem is implicit — the customer would not be in this market segment if they had a tangible problem to solve. Internal and philosophical problems remain central, but expressed in aesthetic register rather than utility register.

Example:
- Mass-market: "You're tired of cheap watches that break after a year." (external)
- Luxury: "An instrument that respects the gravity of the time it measures." (philosophical, implicit external)

### 3.3 Guide

**Standard SB7:** Empathy ("we understand") + Authority ("we have credentials").
**Luxury overlay:** Authority is **structural**, not performed. House heritage, founding lineage, atelier provenance, materiel mastery — these are referenced through artifacts, not claims. Empathy is replaced by **kinship** — the brand and customer share a sensibility, not a transaction.

Derval's rule: a luxury brand never tells you it understands; it demonstrates that it shares your assumptions.

### 3.4 Plan

**Standard SB7:** Clear process steps reducing customer friction.
**Luxury overlay:** The plan is **ritualized** rather than streamlined. Bespoke fitting, atelier visit, founder consultation, multi-year waiting list — these are not friction; they are part of the value proposition.

Plan in luxury is the experience, not the path through it.

### 3.5 Call to Action

**Standard SB7:** Direct CTA ("Buy now") + Transitional CTA ("Download guide").
**Luxury overlay:** Direct CTAs use language of **invitation, audience, appointment**. Transitional CTAs are atelier visits, private viewings, curated correspondence, not lead magnets. "Schedule a viewing" rather than "Get started."

The CTA conveys that engaging with the brand is a privilege earned through the customer's stated interest, not the brand's outreach.

### 3.6 Failure

**Standard SB7:** Stakes of inaction explicit ("Without this, you'll miss out / lose / suffer").
**Luxury overlay:** Failure is **alluded** to, never enumerated. The cost of not engaging is implicit — the customer who reads the narrative understands what they would forgo by walking away. Spelling it out breaks the restraint.

Anti-pattern: "Don't settle for ordinary." (Mentions ordinary, breaks the spell.) Replace with focus on what the customer would gain through engagement, leaving absence as understood.

### 3.7 Success

**Standard SB7:** Vivid description of the resolved future state.
**Luxury overlay:** Success is depicted through **objects, settings, and sensory cues**, not lifestyle outcomes. The watch on the wrist at the perfect dinner. The fabric folded in the atelier drawer. The instrument's case opened to morning light.

These cues invite the customer to project their own narrative onto the artifact, rather than receiving a packaged dream.

## 4. Voice Calibration

| Register | Mass-Market Default | Luxury Overlay |
|----------|---------------------|----------------|
| Sentence length | Short, punchy | Variable, often longer, with measured cadence |
| Word origin | Anglo-Saxon, common | Latinate, French-influenced where meaningful |
| Emphasis pattern | Bold, exclamation, italic | Restraint — emphasis through structure, not typography |
| Tense | Active, present | Present + occasional past perfect (heritage signaling) |
| First person | "We," frequent | "We" rare; house name preferred ("Maison X believes...") |
| Direct address | "You" common | "You" sparing; oblique reference more frequent |
| Imperative | "Discover," "Experience" | "Consider," "Allow yourself" |

---

## 5. Three-Layer Problem Specific to Luxury

| Layer | Mass-Market Framing | Luxury Reframing |
|-------|---------------------|------------------|
| External | "Your current watch is unreliable" | (Implicit — absence of any external problem assumed) |
| Internal | "You feel undervalued" | "You feel the gap between everyday encounters and the few that matter" |
| Philosophical | "You deserve better" | "There is a quality of attention the world increasingly does not afford. Some things should refuse to participate in that decline." |

The philosophical layer is **always** elevated in luxury narrative. It is the entry point, not the conclusion.

---

## 6. Villains in Luxury Narrative

Villains exist but are **abstract** rather than named:
- Ephemera (the disposable economy)
- Distraction (attention-fragmenting culture)
- Compromise (the accepted defeat of standards)
- Mediocrity (the average as default)
- The clock (time as the universal antagonist)

Never name competitors. Never name customer pain points concretely. The villain is what the customer's sensibility implicitly opposes.

## 7. IMC Plan Modifications for Luxury

**Standard IMC Reimagined:** Internal storyteller program + external multi-channel diffusion.
**Luxury overlay:**

| IMC Element | Standard | Luxury Overlay |
|-------------|---------|----------------|
| Internal storyteller program | All employees as ambassadors | Selective — atelier staff, sales associates, customer relations only; brand kept internal-elevated |
| Owned channels | Website, blog, email, social | Website + selective email + private correspondence; minimal social |
| Earned channels | PR pitches to broad press | Strategic placement in heritage trade publications + selected lifestyle press |
| Paid channels | Performance marketing | Programmatic eschewed; only brand-prestige placements (back covers, full-page, never banner) |
| Shared (social) | Active community building | Curated community — invitation, not opt-in |
| Events | Activations, popups | Atelier visits, private salons, trunk shows, owner events |

## 8. Robin-to-Batman Test (Luxury Modifier)

The standard Robin-to-Batman test asks: is the customer the hero (Batman) and the brand the Guide (Alfred)?

**Luxury modifier:** Both the customer and the brand are hero-adjacent. The relationship is more like **two connoisseurs in conversation** than guide-and-hero. The brand neither subordinates itself (mass-market Alfred deference) nor elevates itself above the customer (anti-luxury aspirational distance). The narrative seats both parties at the same table.

When auditing the BrandScript for a luxury brand, ask: does this read like two people of equal sophistication discussing something they both love? If yes, pass. If the brand sounds like a servant or a teacher, fail — restraint discipline broken.

---

## 9. Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Aspirational language**: "Become the person you've always wanted to be." (Implies customer is currently lacking.)
- **Pain point enumeration**: "Tired of mass-produced watches?" (Mentions mass-produced — breaks restraint.)
- **Direct comparisons**: "Better than any Rolex." (Naming competitors is anti-luxury.)
- **Performance of empathy**: "We know how busy your life is." (Replace with implicit understanding.)
- **Urgency tactics**: "Limited time offer." (Use scarcity through actual scarcity, not urgency language.)
- **Demographic framing**: "Perfect for the executive on the go." (Replace with sensibility framing.)
