# Robin-to-Batman Test — Hard Quality Gate

Reference loaded for every BrandScript. The single most-failed test in brand storytelling.

## The doctrine (Miller / Rodriguez)

> *In every effective brand story, the customer is Batman and the brand is Robin.*

The brand exists to support the customer's heroism — not to perform its own. Brands consistently fail this test because the people building brands love their brands and want them to be the heroes of the story.

The discipline is to resist the temptation to make the brand the hero.

## Why brands fail this test

### Temptation 1: Founder ego

The founder built the brand. They are proud. Their story (their journey, their struggle, their triumph) is genuinely compelling. The temptation is to make the founder's story the brand story.

This is acceptable in founder profiles, founder interviews, founder essays. It is NOT acceptable in the BrandScript. The BrandScript is for the customer, not the founder.

### Temptation 2: Differentiation pressure

The brand is being launched. Marketing leadership wants the brand to stand out. Standing out feels easiest by saying "we are the best", "we are the only", "we are the heroes". The temptation is to write a self-promotional BrandScript.

This is acceptable in feature comparison pages, capability matrices, RFP responses. It is NOT acceptable in the BrandScript.

### Temptation 3: Achievement pride

The brand has real achievements — customer outcomes, awards, certifications, scale. The temptation is to lead with these.

Achievements belong in the "authority" sub-section of the Guide element. They do not belong as the hero of the story.

### Temptation 4: Category convention

In some categories (luxury, financial services, professional services) the convention has historically been brand-as-hero — "we are the leaders", "we are the trusted experts", "we are the premier institution". The temptation is to follow convention.

Convention is changing. Customers across categories increasingly tune out brand-as-hero narratives. Customer-as-hero brands consistently outperform.

## The three tests

### Test 1: Hero identification

Read the BrandScript aloud. Then ask:

> *"Who is the hero of this story?"*

If the answer is the brand: FAIL.
If the answer is the customer: PASS.

This sounds simple. It is brutally telling. Most BrandScripts produced under brand-team pressure fail this test on first reading.

### Test 2: Sidekick versus Batman

Read the BrandScript focusing on the brand's role. Then ask:

> *"Is the brand acting as Robin or as Batman?"*

Robin signals:
- The brand provides guidance, tools, support
- The brand makes the customer's actions effective
- The brand's competence serves the customer's competence
- The brand's success is downstream of the customer's success

Batman signals:
- The brand performs the heroism
- The customer is positioned as recipient, not actor
- The brand's competence is the point
- The brand's success is the focus

### Test 3: Stranger identification

The most powerful test. Give the BrandScript to someone who:
- Does not work at the brand
- Has no prior knowledge of the brand
- Fits the target persona

Ask them: *"After reading this, who in the story did you find yourself identifying with?"*

If they identify with the brand (or with the founder, or with the team): the BrandScript is brand-as-hero — FAIL.
If they identify with the customer character: the BrandScript is customer-as-hero — PASS.

This test reveals the truth. Brand teams cannot diagnose their own brand-as-hero drift because they identify with the brand professionally. Strangers cannot identify with the brand professionally — they only have the text. They will tell you who the text is centering.

## Common BrandScript failure patterns

### Failure pattern 1: "We have a problem"

The BrandScript opens with the brand's mission or struggle.

> *Bad*: "Our brand exists because the industry is broken."
> *Good*: "The customer who is [persona], wants [want]. They face [external problem]."

### Failure pattern 2: Achievement-stacked Guide

The Guide section becomes an awards parade.

> *Bad*: "Our brand has won 11 awards, has been featured in 23 publications, has served 500 customers, and is the leading [category] firm in the region."
> *Good*: "Our brand has accumulated [authority evidence] from [empathy evidence — why we know this struggle]."

Achievements appear, but framed as service capacity, not bragging rights.

### Failure pattern 3: Brand-centric transformation

The Success section describes what the brand achieves, not what the customer experiences.

> *Bad*: "Our brand transforms the industry by setting new standards of [thing]."
> *Good*: "Within [timeframe], the customer experiences [concrete transformation in their own life]."

### Failure pattern 4: Possessive Plan

The Plan describes what the brand does, not what the customer does.

> *Bad*: "Step 1: We assess your needs. Step 2: We design a solution. Step 3: We deliver."
> *Good*: "Step 1: You complete a 10-minute assessment. Step 2: You receive a tailored proposal within 48 hours. Step 3: You begin work in 14 days."

Same activities — but reframed from customer's perspective. The plan is what the customer experiences, not what the brand performs.

### Failure pattern 5: Brand-bragging stakes

The Failure stakes describe what customers lose by not choosing the brand, framed as proof of the brand's superiority.

> *Bad*: "Without our brand, you'll be stuck with inferior alternatives."
> *Good*: "Without intentional craft, your daily ritual remains anonymous — coffee from beans you don't know, roasted you-don't-know-when, brewed for a customer you-could-be-anyone."

The stakes are honest portrayals of inaction, not insults to alternatives.

## How to remediate a failed BrandScript

If the Robin-to-Batman test fails:

### Step 1: Identify the failure pattern

Use the failure patterns above. Most failed BrandScripts exhibit one dominant pattern.

### Step 2: Rewrite from customer perspective

Re-read every paragraph and ask:
- Whose perspective is this written from?
- Who is the actor in this sentence?
- Who experiences the outcome?

Rewrite until the customer is consistently the actor and experiencer.

### Step 3: Restrict brand-as-actor to the Guide section

The brand appears as actor only in the Guide section. Elsewhere, the brand is the supporting context that enables the customer's action.

### Step 4: Re-test

Run all three tests again. If still failing, the brand may have a deeper positioning issue (positioning that genuinely centers the brand rather than the customer). Return to brand-platform for positioning rework.

## Robin-to-Batman in B2B contexts

B2B brands sometimes argue: "Our customers buy because we are the experts." This is true. AND the BrandScript still centers the customer.

The B2B Guide can demonstrate stronger authority than a consumer brand Guide — citations, methodology, track record. But the brand still acts in service of the customer's goals, not in performance of its own expertise.

A B2B BrandScript that opens "We are the leading firm in [category]" fails Robin-to-Batman just as a consumer BrandScript would.

## Robin-to-Batman in luxury contexts

Luxury brands sometimes argue: "Our customers buy because of our heritage and prestige." This is true. AND the BrandScript still centers the customer.

The luxury Guide can demonstrate heritage and prestige. But the customer experiences the luxury — they are the actor in the relationship. The brand provides the context (heritage, craft, atelier) that the customer enters.

A luxury BrandScript that opens "Since 1847 we have set the standard for [category]" fails Robin-to-Batman. The customer's experience must lead.

In the BrandScript document, the Robin-to-Batman test results appear as:

```markdown
## 8. Robin-to-Batman Compliance Test

### Test 1: Hero identification
**Question**: Who is the hero of this story?
**Answer**: <Customer / Brand>
**Result**: PASS / FAIL

### Test 2: Sidekick vs Batman
**Question**: Is the brand Robin or Batman?
**Answer**: <Robin / Batman>
**Result**: PASS / FAIL

### Test 3: Stranger identification (if conducted)
**Question**: Whom does an unaffiliated reader identify with?
**Answer**: <Customer / Brand>
**Result**: PASS / FAIL / NOT TESTED

### Overall
**Result**: PASS / FAIL
**Remediation notes (if FAIL)**: <description>
```

A BrandScript with any FAIL must not proceed downstream until remediated.
