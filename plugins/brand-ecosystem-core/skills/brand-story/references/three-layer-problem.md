# Three-Layer Problem Analysis — Mandatory Reference

Loaded at Step 0. The most consequential element of the SB7 framework deserves dedicated treatment.

## The thesis

Customers face problems on three layers simultaneously. Brands that address only the external problem compete on features and price. Brands that address the internal and philosophical problems achieve emotional differentiation and category-redefining positioning.

The three layers, per Miller:
1. **External problem** — observable obstacle in the world
2. **Internal problem** — emotional frustration that obstacle creates
3. **Philosophical problem** — "this is wrong in the world" stance

## Layer 1 — External Problem

### Definition

The thing the customer can point at. The complaint they make out loud. The observable obstacle to their goal.

### Construction discipline

State the external problem:
- In the customer's vocabulary, not the brand's
- As something the customer says aloud, not something the brand observes
- Specifically enough that the customer would recognize "yes, that's me"
- Without product references (the customer's problem exists independently of any product solution)

### Examples by category

**Specialty coffee**:
External: "It's hard to find coffee that tastes consistently good — fresh, well-roasted, brewed with intention."

**B2B consulting**:
External: "We make major strategic decisions without enough rigorous information to be confident."

**Design studio**:
External: "Our brand looks like every other brand in our category."

**Luxury jewelry**:
External: "I can't find jewelry that's distinctive without being ostentatious."

**Hospitality**:
External: "Travel for work has become exhausting because hotels feel interchangeable."

### Failure modes

| Failure | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Brand-vocabulary problem | "Customers face suboptimal coffee quality" | Rewrite in customer's voice |
| Product-bound problem | "Customers need a better coffee subscription" | The customer's problem exists before the solution |
| Vague problem | "Customers want better quality" | Specify what "quality" means concretely |
| Over-specific problem | "Customers can't find coffee from the Yirgacheffe region delivered within 7 days of roast" | This is solution language, not problem language |

## Layer 2 — Internal Problem

### Definition

The emotional consequence of the external problem. What the customer feels but rarely articulates. The reason the external problem matters.

### Why this layer is decisive

Customers don't buy products. Customers buy emotional state-change. The internal problem is the emotional state the customer wants to escape.

A specialty coffee brand selling beans is selling the absence of disappointment + the presence of intentionality. The beans are instrumental to the emotional outcome.

### Construction discipline

State the internal problem:
- As a feeling (frustration, disappointment, anxiety, embarrassment, dread, resignation)
- Connected causally to the external problem
- Without judgment of the customer for feeling it
- Specifically — different external problems generate different internal feelings

### Common internal problem patterns

| External problem pattern | Common internal feeling |
|---|---|
| Inconsistent quality | Disappointment, slight foolishness for paying premium and getting ordinary |
| Decision paralysis | Anxiety, dread of choosing wrong |
| Time-cost | Resentment, frustration with friction |
| Category indistinguishability | Frustration, sense of being treated as just another customer |
| Self-perception risk | Embarrassment, anxiety about social signaling |
| Compound cost of inaction | Dread, anxiety about long-term consequences |
| Skill or knowledge gap | Inadequacy, embarrassment about not knowing |

### Examples (continuing the coffee brand)

External: "Hard to find coffee that tastes consistently good."
Internal: "The customer feels disappointed when they pay premium prices for ordinary coffee. They feel slightly foolish for having been promised something they didn't receive. They begin to mistrust the language of 'specialty' altogether."

### The internal-problem test

Read your internal problem statement to someone who fits your target persona. They should respond with recognition: "Yes — that's exactly how I feel." If they respond with intellectual agreement but no emotional recognition ("yes, I suppose that's a problem"), the internal problem is intellectualized and won't drive behavior.

## Layer 3 — Philosophical Problem

### Definition

The "this is wrong in the world" stance the brand takes. A position on why the category's status quo is unacceptable.

### Why this layer matters

The philosophical layer is the brand's broader argument. It's what makes the brand worth paying attention to beyond its immediate product utility. It's the source of long-form content, manifesto copy, and the social/cultural footprint of the brand.

### Construction discipline

State the philosophical problem as a critique of:
- A category-wide pattern that fails customers
- A cultural assumption that needs challenging
- A systemic failure the brand stands against
- A loss of value that the brand seeks to restore

### Pattern templates

**Restoration philosophy** ("This used to be better; we're restoring it"):
- "Coffee — like everything else — has been industrialized to mediocrity. Customers deserve roasters who know where the beans came from."
- "Tailoring used to be a relationship between maker and wearer. Mass production erased that relationship. We're rebuilding it."

**Critique philosophy** ("The category lies to customers; we tell the truth"):
- "Most 'specialty' coffee isn't specialty — it's marketing. We use the term only when the beans earn it."
- "Most consultancy sells confidence. We sell rigor."

**Possibility philosophy** ("Customers don't know what's possible; we'll show them"):
- "Most people have never tasted coffee at its best. We're going to change that one cup at a time."
- "Most brands have never seen what their identity could become with sustained attention. We're going to show them."

### Examples

For the specialty coffee brand:
> "Coffee — like everything else in modern life — has been industrialized to the point of mediocrity. The customer deserves to know who roasted their beans, where they came from, and why. The 'specialty' label has been so casually applied that it has lost meaning. We use it only when the beans earn it, and we tell customers exactly why."

For a craft jewelry brand:
> "Luxury jewelry has fractured into two extremes: monogram-shouting status pieces, or anonymous mass-luxury that signals nothing. The customer who wants something distinctive without being ostentatious — who treats jewelry as personal language rather than social code — has been left without a home. We exist for that customer."

### Failure modes

| Failure | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract philosophy | "We believe in quality" | State the critique specifically |
| Self-righteous philosophy | "We're the only honest brand" | Acknowledge complexity; don't moralize |
| Disconnected philosophy | Philosophy doesn't connect to external/internal problems | Ensure the three layers are causally linked |
| Brand-bragging philosophy | Philosophy is really self-promotion | Reframe as critique of category, not promotion of brand |

## How the Three Layers Connect

The three layers are causally linked:

```
Philosophical problem (the world is broken in this way)
        ↓ produces
External problem (the customer encounters this obstacle)
        ↓ produces
Internal problem (the customer feels this emotion)
```

A coherent three-layer analysis reads bottom-up or top-down without breaking. Test:
- Bottom-up: "Customer feels X. Because they encounter Y. Because the world is broken in way Z."
- Top-down: "The world is broken in way Z. Which produces obstacle Y. Which produces feeling X."

If the chain breaks, the layers are not yet coherent.

## How the Three Layers Inform Downstream

### To brand-maker

The internal + philosophical layers inform name semantics. A brand whose philosophical layer is "restoration of craft" leans toward heritage-evocative names. A brand whose philosophical layer is "critique of category" leans toward declarative or oppositional names.

### To brand-visual

The internal layer dictates the emotional register of visual identity. A brand addressing "disappointment + slight foolishness" produces visual identity that feels honest, plain-spoken, evidenced — not glossy. A brand addressing "anxiety about choosing wrong" produces visual identity that feels considered, careful, reassuring.

### To brand-touchpoint

The external layer dictates which touchpoints matter most. A brand whose external problem is "interchangeable hotel rooms" must invest heavily in the room-as-touchpoint. A brand whose external problem is "decision paralysis" must invest in the consultation-as-touchpoint.

### To brand-launch

The philosophical layer is the press release. The internal layer is the customer testimonial. The external layer is the product feature. Each layer enters the launch at a different communication tier.

## Bilingual Discipline

When the BrandScript is produced in TR, all three layers are translated with strategic — not literal — equivalence:

```
Dışsal problem (External): <statement in TR>
İçsel problem (Internal): <statement in TR>  
Felsefi problem (Philosophical): <statement in TR>
```

Cultural specificity in the internal layer often requires adaptation, not translation. The feeling-words available in TR don't always map 1:1 to EN. Translator must capture the *emotional valence*, not the word-for-word equivalent.
